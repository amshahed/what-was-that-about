## Psis (telepaths, precogs and others)
- **What it is:** people with psionic talents — telepaths (read minds), precogs (see futures),
  parakineticists, animators, "resurrectors" (ch. 1–2). Most work for **Ray Hollis**, who rents them
  out for industrial espionage.
- **First appears:** ch. 1.
- **How it works in the book:** a precog sees possible futures side by side "like cells in a beehive"
  and picks the brightest one (ch. 3). Telepaths' strength is measured in "blr units" (ch. 1).
- **Introduce when:** setup.
- **Visual idea:** a corporate spy reading an office worker's mind over his shoulder.

## Anti-psis / inertials and prudence organizations
- **What it is:** people whose talent cancels a psi talent — the "inertials". Firms like Runciter
  Associates rent them out to block psi snooping; the industry calls itself "prudence organizations"
  and is regulated by the Prudence **Society** (ch. 1, 4).
- **First appears:** ch. 1.
- **How it works:** an inertial's field cancels a psi's field; the battle is "Psi by Psi"; Joe
  measures both fields (ch. 4, 6). Joe's speech: predator/prey balance — psis prey on normals,
  anti-psis prey on psis (ch. 3). Runciter's ads sell fear: "Is a stranger tuning in on you?" (ch. 1);
  a moratorium owner suspects the whole business may be a racket (ch. 1).
- **Introduce when:** setup (one sentence; it's the job, not the theme).
- **Visual idea:** a security-company ad parody; a "mind-reading" field meter with needles.

## Half-life and cold-pac
- **What it is:** a technology that keeps the recently dead in a kind of dream-consciousness. The body
  is frozen in a transparent casket ("cold-pac") at a **moratorium**; relatives pay to "revive" them
  for a talk through earphone and microphone (ch. 1–2).
- **First appears:** ch. 1.
- **How it works:** each revival spends some of the remaining half-life; the lading sheet shows days
  left; the signal weakens toward the end (ch. 1). Half-lifers placed near each other bleed into each
  other's minds ("osmosis"; ch. 2). There is a holiday, Resurrection Day (ch. 1). Swiss moratoriums
  claim two extra hours of half-life on average (ch. 7).
- **Introduce when:** setup — the whole twist depends on it.
- **Visual idea:** a frosted glass casket in a funeral-home "consultation lounge", a relative with a
  headset like a call-center worker.

## The 1992 future (and coin-operated everything)
- **What it is:** Dick's 1969 guess at 1992: colonies on Luna and Mars, vidphones, homeopape machines
  that print newspapers, hovercars — and a hyper-commercial world where doors, fridges, showers and
  coffee pots charge coins (ch. 3, 6).
- **First appears:** ch. 1–3.
- **How it works:** Joe's own front door won't let him out without five cents and threatens to sue
  (ch. 3); a coffee-shop machine threatens police over one poscred (ch. 7). Money is "poscreds".
- **Introduce when:** inline (the door is the perfect cold-open gag).
- **Visual idea:** Joe vs. his door.

## Pat Conley's talent (changing the past)
- **What it is:** she doesn't time-travel; she thinks hard about a past event and the present
  rearranges; only she remembers the old version (ch. 3, 5). Runciter later calls it a counter-process
  that "uncovers the prior stages" in matter (ch. 14) — which is why it looks like the cause of the
  regression.
- **First appears:** ch. 3.
- **Introduce when:** inline, at the hiring scene.
- **Visual idea:** Pat rewrites the morning — Runciter suddenly a retired coin collector on Fifth
  Avenue, then Pat suddenly "Mrs. Chip" (ch. 5).

## The regression (world decay)
- **What it is:** after the blast, objects age and then revert to older forms: stale cigarettes,
  sour cream, obsolete coins, a 1990 phone book (ch. 6–7); a new tape recorder becomes a worn
  40-year-old model (ch. 9); elevators, TVs, cars, planes slide back to 1939 (ch. 9–10). Even Ubik
  regresses (ch. 10, 12).
- **First appears:** ch. 6 (crumbling cigarette).
- **How it works:** Joe borrows Plato — every object keeps its earlier "forms" inside it; when the
  current form wears off, an older one surfaces (ch. 10). Jory later says the regression is the
  natural decay of the fake world he builds when he can't hold it up (ch. 15–16).
- **Introduce when:** inline — it's the recap's engine.
- **Visual idea:** the same object across eras in one frame (our "reality regression" gag).

## The deaths (withering)
- **What it is:** members of the group get tired and cold, crave being alone, and are found as tiny,
  mummified husks (ch. 8, 9, 11–13).
- **How it works:** it's Jory eating what's left of their half-life (ch. 15). The urge to be alone is
  a "tropism" toward death (ch. 13).
- **Introduce when:** inline (Wendy in the closet, ch. 8).

## Runciter's manifestations
- **What it is:** messages from Runciter in impossible places: his voice on a hotel phone, his face
  on coins and money, a matchbook ad, a note in a cigarette carton, men's-room graffiti, a live TV
  ad, a traffic ticket, a drugstore label, skywriting (ch. 8–12).
- **How it works:** Runciter is alive outside, speaking into the moratorium's microphone; only words
  can get in from outside (ch. 14–15).
- **Introduce when:** inline — this is the mystery thread.
- **Visual idea:** the graffiti couplet on a bathroom wall (code-kit text beat).

## Ubik
- **What it is (on the surface):** a product. Every chapter opens with an ad for Ubik as something
  different — used car, beer, coffee, salad dressing, razor, bank, hair conditioner, deodorant,
  toaster pastry, bra, plastic wrap, cereal… always "safe when used as directed" (ch. 1–16).
- **In the plot:** a spray can that stops the decay and restores half-lifers (ch. 13, 15–16). Its
  "tech" is mock-science: a negative ionizer that slows "anti-protophasons" (ch. 16). Made by
  half-lifers threatened by Jory, mainly **Ella** (ch. 16).
- **The name:** from Latin *ubique*, "everywhere" (ch. 10, 11).
- **At the end:** the final epigraph is not an ad; Ubik speaks as God — it made the suns and the
  worlds, it is the word, and "Ubik" is not its real name (ch. 17).
- **Introduce when:** tease in the cold open (an ad), explain at the twist (ch. 16), land in analysis.
- **Visual idea:** a recurring parody-ad bumper between sections (code-kit text-hero + an AI can).

## The Bardo Thodol (Tibetan Book of the Dead)
- **What it is:** Runciter tells Ella that her smoky red light is a bad "womb" for rebirth (ch. 2);
  Al looks for the lights as he dies (ch. 9); Ella will be reborn soon (ch. 16).
- **Introduce when:** analysis (one line), or inline with Ella.

## Moratorium politics: "Jorys in every moratorium"
- **What it is:** Jory's family pays von Vogelsang to keep him near other half-lifers; Ella says every
  moratorium has a Jory and the fight happens "on our side of the glass" (ch. 16).
- **Introduce when:** analysis — capitalism even in the afterlife.

## Luna and the trap
- **What it is:** Runciter's rule: be suspicious of any job off Earth, especially Luna, where civil law
  has a loophole (ch. 7). The "Stanton Mick" job is a trap; Joe measures no psi field at all (ch. 6).
- **Introduce when:** inline.
